## In 3 lines

- **What it is:** Measuring AI value means comparing business results with the AI against a baseline without it, then subtracting what the AI costs.
- **Why it matters:** Budgets go to projects that can prove their worth, and people's feelings about AI speed are often wrong.
- **Example:** A support team records ticket times for a month, gives the AI assistant to some teams first, and compares them with the rest.

## Think of it like…

<Analogy>
Think of a farmer testing a new fertilizer. They do not spread it on every field and hope. They treat half the field, leave half alone, and weigh both harvests. Then they check if the extra crop is worth more than the fertilizer.

**Where the comparison breaks:** Fields do not change their behavior when watched, but people do. Teams may work harder simply because a new tool is being tested.
</Analogy>

## Real-world example

An online shoe store adds an AI assistant that drafts replies for its support team. After two months, the manager reports that "everyone loves it". Finance asks for the savings, and nobody can answer. No one wrote down how long tickets took before the launch.

For the next project, the architect plans the measurement first. The team agrees on the goal: resolve more tickets per hour without hurting customer satisfaction. They record four weeks of baseline numbers. Then they roll the tool out to one team at a time, so the other teams act as a comparison group.

After eight weeks, they compare tickets per hour, satisfaction scores and the AI's full monthly cost. Now the business case rests on numbers, not on opinions.

## How it works

### 1. Start from a business outcome

A **key performance indicator (KPI)** is a number that tracks progress toward a goal. Microsoft's planning guide says to define specific success measures when you plan each AI project. It words each use case with an expected result, such as "so resolution time drops". "Use AI in support" is not a goal. "Cut average handling time (the average time to close one ticket) without lowering satisfaction" is.

### 2. Measure at several levels

Google Cloud suggests five groups of KPIs. Each group answers a different question.

| Group | Question | Example measure |
|---|---|---|
| Model quality | Are the answers good? | How often answers are accurate and grounded in the source material. |
| System quality | Does it run well? | Speed, errors, uptime (share of time it works). |
| Business operations | Did the work change? | Average handle time, share of chats solved without a human. |
| Adoption | Do people use it? | How many people use it, how often, and their feedback. |
| Business value | Was it worth it? | Productivity, cost savings, growth, customer experience. |

A great model with no users creates no value. High usage with no change in the work creates none either. Each group feeds the next: quality → usage → work change → value. For AI agents, which carry out multi-step tasks on their own, Google adds **time to verify**. That is the time between the agent finishing and a person approving its work. If review takes longer than doing the task by hand, Google says friction outweighs value.

### 3. Record a baseline, then compare fairly

A **baseline** is the measure of how things work today, before the change. A **control group** is a group that does not get the tool, so you can see what would have happened anyway.

A study by Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, published by NBER (the US National Bureau of Economic Research), used this method. It followed 5,179 customer support staff. The AI assistant reached staff at different times, so workers who had it could be compared with workers who did not yet have it. Workers with access solved 14% more issues per hour on average. Newer, less-skilled workers gained 34%, while the most experienced barely changed. An average can hide big differences between groups.

### 4. Do not trust feelings

METR, an AI research group, ran a **randomized controlled trial**: tasks were assigned by chance to "AI allowed" or "AI not allowed". In early 2025, 16 experienced developers took 19% longer with AI. Yet afterwards, they believed AI had sped them up by about 20%.

METR stresses this was a snapshot of early-2025 tools in one setting. Its 2026 update found that many developers dropped out or held back tasks because they did not want to work without AI. The people who believed in AI most were missing, so METR called its newer numbers unreliable and is changing its study design. The lesson for architects is not "AI is slow". It is that surveys of how people feel are no substitute for measured results.

### 5. Turn results into a business case

**Return on investment (ROI)** compares the gain to the cost: (benefit − cost) ÷ cost. Count the full cost: model usage, building, support and staff time spent checking outputs. A **business case** is the argument, backed by numbers, for whether a project is worth funding. Google's agent guide suggests **cost per successful task**, which joins cost and quality in one number. For example, a run that costs 10 cents but succeeds only half the time really costs 20 cents per success.

```text
baseline (before) ──► tool to group A, not to group B
                               │
                               ▼
      compare A vs B on KPIs: quality → usage → work change → value
                               │
                               ▼
          benefit − full cost ──► keep, fix or stop
```

## Common mistakes

- **No baseline** — without "before" numbers, nobody can prove a change. Measure for a few weeks before launch.
- **Counting usage as value** — many logins do not mean better work. Link usage to a business outcome, such as tickets solved.
- **Trusting surveys alone** — people can feel faster while being slower. Pair surveys with measured times and results.
- **Ignoring part of the cost** — leaving out review time or support makes ROI look better than it is. Include every cost that the project adds.
- **Reporting only the average** — a 14% average can hide a group that gains a lot and a group that gains little. Break results down by team or skill level.

## Remember this

<Remember>
- Pick a business outcome and its KPIs before you build, and record a baseline before launch.
- Compare against a group without the tool, and measure the chain from quality to usage to work change to value.
- Feelings about speed can be wrong, so ROI must rest on measured results and the full cost.
</Remember>
