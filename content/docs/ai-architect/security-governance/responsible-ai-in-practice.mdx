## In 3 lines

- **What it is:** Responsible AI in practice means turning values like fairness, transparency and accountability into design rules, tests, documents and sign-offs.
- **Why it matters:** A principle on a poster changes nothing. A rule with a test and an owner can stop a harmful system before it ships.
- **Example:** "Be fair" becomes "approval rates for each age group must stay within a set gap, checked before launch and every month."

## Think of it like…

<Analogy>
Think of a building code. "Buildings should be safe" is a value. The code turns it into checks: exits every so many meters, wiring that passes inspection, and an inspector who signs off. Responsible AI does the same for values like fairness.

**Where the comparison breaks:** Building codes have exact numbers set by law. For AI fairness, your team often has to choose the measure and the limit itself.
</Analogy>

## Real-world example

In this made-up example, an online store adds an AI model that decides which shoppers see a "pay later" offer. The offer lets shoppers split a purchase into payments. Shoppers who do not see it lose a real benefit.

The team writes three rules. **Fairness:** compare offer rates and error rates (how often the model gets it wrong) across age groups before launch, and keep the gap under a limit they agreed on. **Transparency:** publish a short document on what the model is for and how it was tested. Tell shoppers why they did or did not get the offer. **Accountability:** name one manager who owns the model, log every decision, and give shoppers a way to ask a human to review it.

At the pre-launch review, the test shows shoppers over 60 get the offer far less often. The owner blocks the launch until the team finds the cause: the training data (the past examples the model learned from) had very few older shoppers.

## How it works

### Where the principles come from

The **Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD)** is an international group of governments. It adopted its AI Principles in 2019 and updated them in May 2024. Its five principles about values include fairness (under human rights and democratic values), transparency and explainability, and accountability. **Explainability** means being able to say why the system gave a result. Many company and government frameworks use similar words. The hard part is what comes next.

### The three principles as design work

| Principle | Plain meaning | Design requirement | Proof you can show |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fairness** | The system does not harm some groups more than others. | Measure results for each group, not just the overall average. Set a limit for the gap. | A test report with results per group. |
| **Transparency** | People can find out what the system does and how it was built. | Document the data and the model. Tell users when AI affects them, and why. | A datasheet and a model card (both explained below), and a user notice. |
| **Accountability** | A named person answers for the system and can fix it. | Assign an owner. Log decisions. Add an appeal path and a sign-off gate: a step where the owner must approve before launch. | An owner's name, logs, and a signed review. |

### Fairness: first name the harm

The Fairlearn guide (Fairlearn is an open-source fairness toolkit) judges fairness by its impact on people. It lists four kinds of harm:

- **Allocation harm:** the system gives or holds back a chance, a resource or information, like a loan or a job interview.
- **Quality-of-service harm:** the system works worse for some people, like speech recognition that misses certain accents.
- **Stereotyping harm:** the system repeats stereotypes, for example search autocomplete that finishes a query with a stereotype.
- **Erasure harm:** the system acts as if a group does not exist.

Then measure with a **disaggregated** test, meaning results split by group. The model cards paper asks for exactly this: report results per group, so problems in one group are not hidden. For example, a model can be 95% accurate overall but only 70% accurate for shoppers over 60.

Bias does not only come from data, either. NIST's bias report (NIST is the US standards agency) names three categories of bias:

- **Systemic:** unfair habits and rules of institutions.
- **Statistical and computational:** samples that do not match the real population.
- **Human:** thinking errors in the people who build or use the system.

### Transparency: two documents

- A **datasheet for datasets** records why a dataset was made, what it contains, how it was collected, and what it should be used for. The idea copies the spec sheets that come with electronic parts.
- A **model card** is a short document that ships with a model. It states the intended use, how the model was tested, and how it performs for different groups.

### Accountability: review steps with owners

```text
1. Idea      → impact check: who could be harmed, and which harm type?
2. Design    → write rules with metrics and limits; name the owner
3. Build     → datasheet + model card; per-group tests
4. Launch    → review gate: owner signs off, or blocks
5. Run       → monitor per-group results; handle appeals; re-review on change
```

Each step leaves a record. When something goes wrong, you can trace who decided what, and why.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing values with no tests** — "we value fairness" cannot pass or fail. Turn each value into a metric, a limit and a check date.
- **Only checking the average** — a model can be 95% accurate overall and fail one group badly. Always split results by group.
- **Expecting one perfect fairness metric** — Fairlearn notes that two common metrics, demographic parity and equalized odds, sometimes cannot both be met. No math settles which is right. Pick the measure that fits the harm, and write down why in a short decision record.
- **Owners in name only** — if the owner cannot block a launch, nobody is accountable. Give the owner real power at the review gate.
- **Reviewing once and forgetting** — data and users change after launch. Re-run per-group tests on a schedule and after every model change.

## Remember this

<Remember>
- Each principle needs a design requirement, a test or document as proof, and a named owner.
- Fairness starts by naming the harm (allocation, quality of service, stereotyping, erasure), then measuring results per group.
- Datasheets and model cards make AI transparent; logs, appeals and a sign-off gate make it accountable.
</Remember>
